# ADR 0030: WP 3.3 performance hardening

Status: accepted, 2026-09-10. Two hundred tabs, a ten megabyte file, and
what the profiler found in each.

The plan's line: "Profile on the 10 MB file and on a 200-tab session. Fix
what the profiler shows. Tighten CI thresholds to the measured numbers
minus margin so regressions fail the build."

Three things, and the first of them had to be built before it could be
done. The harness measured one large document three ways — first paint,
keystroke latency, the frontend half of the semantic diff — and nothing
at all about a window with many documents in it. A session of two hundred
tabs is the opposite shape of problem, and it was unmeasured.

## What the profiler found

Two new benches, both of them driving the real shell rather than a copy
of it: `session.bench.test.ts` restores, switches through and closes two
hundred tabs, and `switch.bench.test.ts` opens one document at three
sizes and changes how it is shown.

A restore of two hundred tabs took **a second** on an idle machine, and
three quarters of that second was the word count. Every tab a restore put
in the strip was brought to the front on the way past, and coming to the
front counts the words of the document in front — so a launch counted
every word of every document in the session, to show a number about one
of them. It also asked the parser to finish each document first, with a
thirty millisecond budget it could not meet on anything over a megabyte,
and threw that work away.

The bridge told the same story from the other side. A restore made a
thousand calls into Rust and sent **five point eight megabytes** back
across it: every document, returned to the side that had just read it, so
that a history snapshot could be taken of a version it already had. The
watcher then read each file a **second** time to find its baseline.

On the ten megabyte file, a word count cost 180 ms and ran on a timer
every quarter of a second while somebody typed.

## A count belongs to the buffer

The fix for most of that is one idea. A word count and an outline are
questions about a buffer, and a buffer is immutable, so the answers are
too: `Doc` now keeps what has been read out of one version of itself and
hands it back rather than reading it again. Two tabs on one document
share it. Going back to a tab costs nothing measurable — the bench reads
0.1 ms, against 4 ms before and a budget of 4.

The memo is keyed by the buffer *and* by how far the parse had reached,
because both answers depend on both: only the tree can tell a comment
from a `<!--` inside a fence, and a heading the parse has not reached is
not in the list yet. So the answers improve as the parser advances and
stand still when it does not. The one subtlety is that asking the parser
to finish can itself move it on, which would leave the memo stale the
moment it was made; `parseTree` writes the new position back, so the
outline's ask finds the word count's work rather than paying for it again.

The forced parse is now asked once and not once per keystroke. What says
whether it is worth asking again is how much is **left** to parse rather
than how long the document is: typing a character adds one to each and
changes nothing, while the editor's own parser getting further, or the
document getting shorter, leaves less to do than there was. That took the
thirty milliseconds off every count of every document over a megabyte.

And a restore now builds the strip instead of walking it. `insert` grew a
`focus` flag, false in exactly one place, and the window goes to one tab
at the end as it always did.

Together: **a restore of two hundred tabs went from about a second to
about a fifth of one**, and the frontend suite still passes unchanged
apart from the tests that asserted the old mechanism.

## What needs the file belongs where the file is

`open_document` used to hand back bytes and metadata. The window then
asked Rust to take a snapshot — sending the whole document back — and to
watch the file, which read it again from disk. Three operations, two
extra crossings, one extra read, for a file that had just been read.

They are now one command. The traffic of a two hundred tab restore fell
from a thousand calls and five point eight megabytes to **four hundred
and one calls and six kilobytes** — the paths, and nothing else. The
image scope is asked for once per folder rather than once per file, which
is where two hundred of those calls went.

It also closed a gap that had nothing to do with speed. A writer landing
between the read and the watch left the window holding one version and
the watcher calling the *next* one the baseline, so the difference
between the two was never reported to anybody. `watch_known` takes the
version the caller was handed, and `crates/core/tests/watcher.rs` has the
case.

Neither the snapshot nor the watch can refuse a file: a history that
cannot be written is not a reason not to open a document, so both
failures are dropped. A file too large to edit is still not recorded —
nothing in the app can change it, so there would never be a second
version to compare the first with.

## The one this package could not fix

Typing into the middle of a ten megabyte document costs seventy to a
hundred and twenty milliseconds a keystroke. At the top of the same
document it costs two.

None of it is this app's. Taking the extension list apart one extension
at a time puts all of it in `markdownSupport` — CodeMirror's own
incremental parse, mapping a tree of several million characters through
every change. It does not settle if you type slowly: it gets *worse* as
more of the document is parsed, because there is more tree to map. There
is no size knob, and forcing the parse to finish in the background would
make it worse rather than better.

Design 8 lets a file of ten megabytes be edited. Design 12 promises
sixteen milliseconds a keystroke. At that size the two do not both hold,
and which of them gives is not a decision a bench can make. The ten
megabyte row of the keystroke bench therefore carries a budget that is
not a promise kept but a measurement written down, and says so.

The word count at that size is the app's own and is also as small as it
is going to get without counting incrementally: about a hundred
milliseconds to walk ten megabytes of text with a Unicode word regex. So
the count now waits in proportion to what it last cost — a fifth of the
time and no more — which leaves an ordinary document counted a quarter of
a second after the last keystroke exactly as before, and a very long one
counted less often rather than the window stopping for it four times a
second.

## Thresholds that fail the build

The harness was not in CI at all, which made design 12's "measured in CI
with thresholds that fail the build" untrue. It is now a job, on macOS
only: these are measurements, and the same harness on three different
runners would be three sets of numbers under one set of thresholds.

Where the design states a promise, that promise is the threshold — a
tenth of a second to first paint at a megabyte, sixteen milliseconds a
keystroke. Where it does not, the threshold is what the harness measures
with room over the top, which is what plan 7.4 asks of a shared runner:
catch a disaster, do not flake on a busy machine. Several that were round
numbers with nothing behind them are now tighter: first paint at a
hundred kilobytes from 100 ms to 50, at a megabyte to 60; a screenful of
scrolling from 16 ms to 8; the diff scan from 75 ms to 45.

The harness also runs one file at a time now. Five bench files racing two
browsers for one machine measures the machine's load: the same numbers
moved by three to twenty times between a bench run alone and the same
bench run beside the others, which is not a property of the code.

## What was left

- **The word count is not incremental.** A keystroke changes one place
  and the count walks the whole document. Doing it properly means
  counting the changed region in the old and new text and adjusting,
  which is tractable but has to get the word boundaries at the edges
  right and has an awkward case: an unclosed `<!--` typed anywhere turns
  the rest of the document into a comment.
- **The outline does not converge on a very long document.** It shows
  what the parse has reached and says so, and nothing pushes the parse
  further in the background. Read mode's own walk is the idiom that would
  do it (plan WP 2.7), applied to the editor.
- **The per-document reading override is still one call a document.** It
  is fire-and-forget, so it does not hold a restore up, and batching it
  needs a new command shape for a win only the real bridge would show.
- **The numbers here are Playwright's Chromium and WebKit**, not
  WKWebView and WebView2. Plan 7.4's `--bench` mode inside the real app
  is what would fix that, and it is still not built.
