# ADR 0021: WP 2.3 Review mode and the history panel

Status: accepted, 2026-09-09. Covers the change record, Review mode and
per-change revert, the measured margin marks, and the history panel
(design 4.4, plan WP 2.3).

WP 2.2 worked out *what* changed. This work package is about showing it
and about being able to say no to it. Design 4.4: "Review mode
(Cmd+Shift+R) walks through changes one at a time with inline old and
new text, like a track-changes view. Each change can be reverted
individually. History panel lists snapshots and lets the user diff any
two or restore one."

## The change record replaces the line run

WP 2.2 handed the editor a list of line runs: three numbers each, enough
to draw a bar. Review mode needs more of every change — the words that
went, the words that came, and the edits that put it back — and the two
lists must not be able to disagree, because they would be describing the
same change.

So the effect carries one thing now. A `ChangeRecord` is a range in the
buffer, the track-changes pieces, and the edits that undo it. The field
holds records and maps them through every edit the reader makes, exactly
as it mapped the decorations before, and everything else is derived: the
margin bars, the panels, the stops the step key walks, the count in the
badge.

That derivation settled a question the line runs had answered by
accident. Three revised list items are one bar in the margin, because
there is no untouched line between them, and they are three things that
happened. They are now three records: three panels, three stops, three
in the count, one bar. The margin says where the document changed; the
walk goes through what changed.

## The record carries its own undo, not a recipe for one

Reverting could have been "work out at the time what this change was and
put it back". It is instead a list of edits computed when the record was
made, where both versions were in hand, and mapped forward with the
record.

The reason is that by the time the reader presses Revert, the only thing
that still knows what the old text said is the record. The buffer has
moved on; the baseline is a snapshot that may itself have been replaced
by a newer write. Carrying the edits means the revert is a transaction
and not a second diff, which also makes it exactly one undo step — the
two halves of putting a moved block back included.

## A block's room is its lines and the separator in front of it

A block's range is not what it occupies. `- two` is a list item whose
paragraph block starts after the marker, and deleting the block's range
would leave a bullet with nothing on it. Taking a paragraph out and
leaving its blank line behind gives the document two blank lines where
it had one.

So every revert works in *room*: the whole lines a block sits on, plus
the whitespace that separated it from the block before it. Taking a
block out takes that separator with it; putting one back brings the
separator it had in the version it came from. Nothing is invented — a
list item comes back with the `\n` that was between it and its
neighbour, a paragraph with the blank line — which is what keeps a
reverted list a list and a reverted table a table.

At the head of the document there is no separator in front, so the
separator behind is used instead and the text carries it on the other
side. Those two cases are the whole of it, and the `reverting` tests in
`changes.test.ts` put every change back and ask for the document they
started from.

## Putting a moved block back needs a neighbour that did not move

A move is the one change whose revert is not local: the block has to
leave where it arrived and return where it was, and "where it was" is a
place in the old document that may not exist in the new one.

The rule is to put it beside the nearest of its old neighbours that is
still in the document *and did not itself move*. A neighbour that moved
says nothing about where "back" is. Where the document holds no such
neighbour the record carries no revert at all, and the panel says so
rather than guessing: there is no answer, and inventing one would put
the text somewhere the reader did not ask for.

## The panel holds both versions; the buffer is left alone

The alternative was track changes over the buffer itself: the added
words marked in place, the removed ones drawn inline as widgets. It is
the nicer picture and it cannot be drawn honestly. The word runs are
offsets into the *normalized* text of a block (ADR 0020), and
normalizing is not offset-preserving, so putting a removed word at the
right place in the buffer would mean carrying a character map for every
block — and the map would be stale the moment the reader typed, because
the runs are from the last scan and the buffer is from now.

The panel shows the two normalized texts with the runs marked in each,
which is exactly what the engine compared. It cannot disagree with the
marks, it does not go wrong when the reader types, and it costs no
mapping. What it gives up is seeing the change in the reader's own
typography, and the buffer two lines below is that.

Untouched text keeps six words at each end of a change and gives up the
middle. A panel is worth reading only if the change is the biggest thing
in it.

## Deletions fold into what replaced them

A block deleted with another arriving in its place is one thing that
happened. WP 2.2 already declined to draw two marks for it. Now the
deleted text goes into the record of what replaced it, as the `gone`
side of a rewrite, and reverting that record puts the old blocks back
where the new one is. A deletion with nothing in its place stays a
record of its own: a notch, with the removed text in the panel, because
that text exists nowhere in the buffer to be pointed at.

## The margin marks are measured, not decorated

The marks were line decorations. CodeMirror draws no lines under a block
widget, so a change inside a table, a fenced block, a math block or an
image that live preview had replaced was drawn nowhere — counted in the
badge, reachable by the step key, invisible in the margin. ADR 0020
recorded the hole and said it belonged here, where change presentation
is the subject. It is worse here: Review mode would have had no panel
for a changed table row.

Both are fixed by asking the view where the position is rather than
which line it is on. The bars are a `layer` of rectangles from
`lineBlockAt`, which answers in blocks and so answers for a widget as
readily as for a line. The panel anchors to the start of the widget
covering its change, because a block widget inside a replaced range is
not drawn either — which is the one place this work package reaches into
live preview, through a named seam rather than a shared field.

The cost is a frame: the bars are drawn in the view's measure phase, so
they land after the text rather than with it. Nobody can see one frame,
and the tests wait for it. The other cost is granularity — inside a
widget the finest thing the view knows about is the widget, so a change
to one row marks the whole table. The panel above it says which row, so
the precision is where it can be read rather than where it can only be
pointed at.

## The comparison base is not the reviewed pointer

The history panel's main gesture is picking a version to measure the
marks against. That could have moved the "last reviewed" pointer, which
is already a version and already what the marks are measured against.

It does not, because they are different questions. What the reader has
seen is advanced by saving and by marking reviewed; what they have asked
to compare with is theirs until they say otherwise, and a save must not
quietly answer it. The document holds both, and the marks are measured
against the comparison when there is one. Marking reviewed does clear
it: the reader has just said they are done, and leaving the marks up
would be arguing.

A comparison is visible from outside the sidebar, which can be shut. The
status bar says `Comparing` and pressing it stops.

## "Diff any two" is a view of one version against the base

Comparing two versions neither of which is the buffer opens the newer
one as a document of its own with the other as its baseline: the whole
change pipeline then applies to it unchanged — the same marks, the same
panels, the same walk. Which other one is the version the panel is
comparing with, or, when there is none, the version just before it,
which is the question a row is usually being asked: what did this one
change?

Such a view is marked ephemeral and left out of the session. It is not a
document to come back to; what it holds is in the history it was opened
out of, and a tab that came back after a restart with no baseline would
be an untitled file with a strange name and no marks.

## Restore keeps what it replaces

Design 4.4: "Restore is itself a user snapshot, so nothing is ever
lost." What is in the buffer becomes a version first, because it may
never have been one — an unsaved edit exists nowhere else, and a restore
that dropped it would be the one way this app can lose text. The restore
itself is an ordinary transaction, so one undo takes it back.

## What was left

**Read mode still shows no changes.** The marks are a CodeMirror layer
and the panels are CodeMirror widgets; Read mode is a different renderer
with neither. Asking for Review from Read switches to Edit, as find and
stepping already do (ADR 0016), so the reader is never told nothing is
there — but a reader who wants to read the changed document rather than
edit it has to leave Read to see what changed.

**The history panel does not say what a version contains.** A row is a
time, a hand and a size. Which parts of the document a version differs
from its neighbour in is a question the engine could answer, and
answering it for every row means a diff per row.

**A version cannot be opened beside the buffer.** Two views of two
versions side by side is a split, and there is no split; the comparison
opens in a tab. WP 2.5's second window is the nearest thing.

**Reverting does not stop autosave.** A revert is an edit like any
other, so the timer writes it a second later. That is the same rule
everything else in the app follows and the undo is one key, but it does
mean a mis-pressed Revert reaches the disk before the reader has looked
at it.
